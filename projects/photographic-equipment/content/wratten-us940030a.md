## The Invention

The patent discloses a plate‑holder designed to secure photographic glass plates during exposure and processing. Its construction permits rapid insertion and removal while maintaining precise alignment with the camera or enlarger. The device incorporates adjustable clamps and a protective frame to reduce breakage of the emulsion‑coated plates. By facilitating consistent positioning, the holder improves both efficiency and image quality in laboratory work.

## The Mark

The WRATTEN trademark is recorded in Class 5 for photographic dry‑plates, safe‑lights, safe‑light lamps, and photographic colour‑screens. The mark therefore identifies a line of consumable photographic materials and associated illumination equipment marketed to professional and amateur photographers. Kodak positioned WRATTEN as a reliable brand for the delicate handling and exposure of dry‑plate emulsions, emphasizing the safety and consistency afforded by its accessories. The registration, filed on 1913‑05‑02, formalized this branding within Kodak’s broader product portfolio.

## The Connection

Both records belong to Kodak's dry‑plate apparatus ecosystem of the early 1910s: the WRATTEN mark covers dry‑plates, safe‑lights, and colour‑screens, and US940030A covers a plate‑holder used to position glass plates during exposure. They share a single owner (Eastman Kodak), a common subject area (dry‑plate photography), and a close timeframe — the trademark was filed about three and a half years after the patent grant. The correspondence is therefore an owner‑, era‑, and subject‑level association rather than a documented product‑patent link: a plate‑holder is a general accessory, and there is no evidence in these records that this particular patent underlies the WRATTEN consumables specifically.

## Historical Context

During the early 1910s Kodak was expanding its dry‑plate system to meet the growing demand for faster, more reliable photographic processes. The introduction of safe‑lights and colour‑screens reflected a broader industry shift toward controlled‑lighting techniques in darkrooms. At the same time, manufacturers sought to differentiate their consumables through brand names, and WRATTEN exemplifies Kodak’s strategy of coupling mechanical innovations with proprietary branding to capture market share. The timing of the trademark filing in 1913 aligns with Kodak’s aggressive promotion of its dry‑plate and illumination technologies ahead of World War I.

## Significance

This patent‑trademark pair illustrates how Kodak translated a specific mechanical invention into a branded product suite, a pattern that recurs throughout its early‑twentieth‑century history. The WRATTEN case demonstrates the interplay between technical improvement (the plate‑holder) and commercial branding (the trademark) in the maturation of the American photographic industry. For the research project, the pair serves as a clear example of a short‑term lag between invention and trademark registration, highlighting how firms leveraged patented technology to justify new brand extensions.
