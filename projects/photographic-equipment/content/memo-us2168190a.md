## The Invention

The patent discloses a photographic enlarging apparatus that integrates a light source, a condenser system, and a movable carriage to hold a negative and a projection lens for controlled enlargement onto photosensitive paper. It emphasizes precise regulation of illumination intensity and focus to improve image sharpness and reduce exposure time. The claims also cover a mechanism for adjusting the distance between the negative and the lens, thereby allowing variable magnification without compromising optical quality. Together, these features constitute a compact, commercially viable enlarger suitable for studio and field use.

## The Mark

The trademark “MEMO” was registered in Class 5 for “photographic goods particularly cameras and films,” indicating that the name was applied to a line of photographic equipment rather than a single device. AGFA ANSCO CORPORATION marketed the MEMO brand as a consumer‑oriented system that combined camera bodies, film, and processing accessories under a unified identity. Contemporary advertisements positioned MEMO as a convenient, all‑in‑one solution for amateur photographers seeking reliable results without professional expertise.

## The Connection

Both records sit within Agfa Ansco's late-1930s photographic line and were dated within sixteen days of each other — the patent granted 1939-08-01, the MEMO trademark filed 1939-08-17 — under the same owner. That tight timing and shared ownership are what make the pair worth surfacing. The correspondence is, however, at the owner-and-era level rather than a documented product link: US2168190A covers photographic enlarging apparatus, while the MEMO mark identifies Ansco's camera and film line, so there is no evidence in these records that this specific patent is embodied in the MEMO products.

## Historical Context

In the late 1930s, the American photographic market was consolidating around a few vertically integrated firms that offered cameras, film, and processing equipment as packaged solutions. AGFA ANSCO, formed after the 1929 acquisition of the American Ansco business by the German AGFA concern, sought to differentiate its products through technical innovation and branding. The MEMO line entered a competitive field dominated by Kodak’s “Brownie” series, and the introduction of an in‑house enlarger reflected a broader industry trend toward providing end‑to‑end photographic services to the growing amateur market.

## Significance

This patent‑trademark pair exemplifies how Ansco used proprietary engineering to create a branded photographic ecosystem, illustrating the interplay between technical invention and commercial identity in the pre‑World War II era. The MEMO case demonstrates that trademark registration can serve as a precise temporal marker for the market introduction of a patented technology, thereby enriching our understanding of product diffusion and corporate strategy during a pivotal period in American photographic history.
