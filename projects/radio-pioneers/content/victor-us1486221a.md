## The Invention

Patent US1486221A, titled "Means For Controlling The Flow Of Electrons In Electric Discharge Devices" and assigned to RCA Corp, was applied for on March 5, 1921, and granted March 11, 1924. It falls in CPC class H01J — electric discharge tubes and discharge lamps. The patent covers electrode arrangements for controlling electron flow within a vacuum tube — the fundamental mechanism that enables radio signal amplification and detection. Radio receivers of the early 1920s depended entirely on vacuum tubes for signal processing; controlling electron flow precisely was the engineering challenge that separated usable radio sets from noise-filled devices. The assignment to RCA Corp reflects the patent pooling arrangement under which RCA, GE, and Westinghouse cross-licensed their radio patents: RCA served as the commercial entity and trademark holder even when individual patents originated from GE or Westinghouse engineers.

## The Mark

VICTOR (Trademark Serial No. 71195203) was filed on April 8, 1924 — twenty-eight days after US1486221A was granted. The filing party was Victor Talking Machine Company, though ownership records also reflect RCA Corporation as a successor, evidencing the corporate transition that followed. The goods statement covers "radio apparatus, parts, and appurtenances — namely, radio receiving sets, sound-amplifying horns having telephone receivers, loud speakers, loops" and related radio equipment. In 1924, the Victor Talking Machine Company was the dominant American phonograph manufacturer; this trademark filing marks its entry into the commercial radio apparatus market. Victor was acquired by RCA in 1929, bringing both the VICTOR trademark and its manufacturing infrastructure into the RCA portfolio.

## The Connection

US1486221A and the VICTOR trademark are linked through the RCA patent pool and the commercial radio apparatus market of 1924. The vacuum tube electron control technology covered by US1486221A was assigned to RCA Corp — the entity that had the exclusive right to manufacture and sell radio sets incorporating pooled patents. Victor Talking Machine Company, which by 1924 had licensed RCA's radio patents to enter the radio market, filed the VICTOR trademark for radio apparatus in the same month that this vacuum tube patent was granted. Both the patent and the trademark thus reflect the same commercial moment: the commercialization of RCA's vacuum tube technology under the VICTOR brand for radio receiving sets.

## Historical Context

April 1924 was a pivotal month in American radio history. The Radio Corporation of America, formed in 1919 under a government-brokered agreement, held pooled radio patents from GE, Westinghouse, AT&T, and United Fruit. RCA had a near-monopoly on radio set manufacturing, which it exercised through licensing arrangements with other manufacturers. Victor Talking Machine Company, facing declining phonograph revenues as radio entertainment displaced the phonograph market, entered the radio set business in 1924, licensing RCA's patent pool. The VICTOR trademark for radio apparatus was filed as part of this market entry. RCA's acquisition of Victor in 1929 consolidated the radio and phonograph businesses under a single company, making both the RCA and VICTOR trademarks part of the same portfolio.

## Significance

This pair captures the pivotal moment when Victor Talking Machine Company — then the world's largest phonograph manufacturer — entered the radio set market using a trademark that had become synonymous with the phonograph. The 28-day gap between patent grant and trademark filing reflects the speed with which commercial radio was developing in 1924. For the radio-pioneers project, this pair documents the mechanism by which the RCA patent pool translated into commercial products: pooled vacuum tube patents (assigned to RCA Corp) were licensed to manufacturers such as Victor, who then filed radio apparatus trademarks to protect their branded product lines. The eventual RCA-Victor merger in 1929 brought this commercial arrangement to its logical conclusion.
