## The Invention

US2168861A claims an improvement to the stroboscopic lamp — an electric discharge tube engineered to generate radiant energy at controlled intervals. The patent, applied for in mid-1934 and granted in August 1939, falls within the H01J classification for electric discharge tubes, a category encompassing cathode-ray devices, gas-filled tubes, and specialized lamps developed for industrial and domestic purposes. The five-year prosecution span reflects both the technical complexity of the device and the patent office's deliberation on novelty claims in an increasingly crowded field of electric discharge innovations.

## The Mark

Westinghouse filed STERILAMP as a word mark on August 25, 1939—seventeen days after the patent grant—announcing a commercial product whose name directly describes its intended function: sterilization through radiation. The goods specification is notably specific: vacuum or gas-filled tubes designed to generate radiations for hastening meat tenderization in retail showcases and domestic refrigerators. This precision reveals not an abstract laboratory concept but a market-ready consumer and commercial application, positioned at the intersection of food preservation and visible product presentation.

## The Connection

The patent and trademark share assignee identity, grant/filing dates within a seventeen-day window, and subject matter alignment: both concern electrically energized discharge tubes engineered to emit controlled radiation. The patent's technical claims regarding stroboscopic frequency and discharge characteristics provided the licensed intellectual foundation for the trademarked product's operation. The zero-year date gap confirms that Westinghouse moved from patent grant to commercial mark registration in the same filing session, indicating immediate commercialization intent.

## Historical Context

In the late 1930s, electric discharge tubes represented the cutting edge of consumer-facing innovation: Westinghouse, General Electric, and other major manufacturers were racing to translate laboratory advances into household and retail applications. Ultraviolet and other non-visible radiations had moved from industrial curiosity to commercial apparatus by the mid-1930s. The application of such technology to meat tenderization—accelerating enzymatic breakdown—positioned Westinghouse within an emerging category of radiation-based food handling, a field that would accelerate dramatically in the postwar period.

## Significance

STERILAMP represents a precise historical moment: the translation of a patented electric discharge innovation into a branded commercial product within weeks of patent grant. The mark's filing in 1939 documents Westinghouse's confidence that radiation-based meat processing was ready for market deployment—whether in butcher showcases or consumer refrigerators. The product itself remained marginal in commercial history, but the pairing exemplifies how major electrical manufacturers converted laboratory patents into consumer-facing marks, and how trademark filings can reveal the product categories companies believed were emerging.
