## The Invention

Patent US1486221A, titled "Means For Controlling The Flow Of Electrons In Electric Discharge Devices" and assigned to RCA Corp, was applied for on March 5, 1921, and granted March 11, 1924. It falls in CPC class H01J — electric discharge tubes and discharge lamps. The patent covers electrode arrangements for controlling electron flow within a vacuum tube — the fundamental mechanism that enables radio signal amplification and detection. Radio receivers of the early 1920s depended entirely on vacuum tubes for signal processing; controlling electron flow precisely was the engineering challenge that separated usable radio sets from noise-filled devices. The assignment to RCA Corp reflects the patent pooling arrangement under which RCA, GE, and Westinghouse cross-licensed their radio patents: RCA served as the commercial entity and trademark holder even when individual patents originated from GE or Westinghouse engineers.

## The Mark

VICTOR (Trademark Serial No. 71195203) was filed on April 8, 1924 — twenty-eight days after US1486221A was granted. The filing party was Victor Talking Machine Company, though ownership records also reflect RCA Corporation as a successor, evidencing the corporate transition that followed. The goods statement covers "radio apparatus, parts, and appurtenances — namely, radio receiving sets, sound-amplifying horns having telephone receivers, loud speakers, loops" and related radio equipment. In 1924, the Victor Talking Machine Company was the dominant American phonograph manufacturer; this trademark filing marks its entry into the commercial radio apparatus market. Victor was acquired by RCA in 1929, bringing both the VICTOR trademark and its manufacturing infrastructure into the RCA portfolio.

## The Connection

US1486221A and the VICTOR trademark are linked through the RCA patent pool and the commercial radio apparatus market of 1924. The vacuum tube electron control technology covered by US1486221A was assigned to RCA Corp — the entity that held the exclusive right to manufacture and sell radio sets incorporating pooled patents. Victor Talking Machine Company, which by 1924 had licensed RCA's radio patents to enter the radio market, filed the VICTOR trademark for radio apparatus in the same month that this vacuum tube patent was granted. Both the patent and the trademark thus reflect the same commercial moment: the translation of RCA's vacuum tube technology into branded radio receiving sets carrying the VICTOR name.

The correspondence is structural rather than direct. Victor Talking Machine Company did not itself hold US1486221A; RCA Corp did. What the 1924-04-08 filing date establishes is that Victor's entry into the radio apparatus market — documented by this trademark application — was premised on access to precisely the class of vacuum tube technology that US1486221A represents. The trademark filing is the commercial surface of a patent-licensing arrangement; the patent is the technical foundation beneath it.

## Historical Context

April 1924 was a pivotal month in American radio history. The Radio Corporation of America, formed in 1919 under a government-brokered agreement, held pooled radio patents from GE, Westinghouse, AT&T, and United Fruit, giving it a near-monopoly on the core technologies required for radio set manufacture. RCA exercised this position through licensing arrangements that permitted selected manufacturers to produce radio apparatus in exchange for royalties — arrangements that simultaneously expanded the market and kept technical control within the pool. Victor Talking Machine Company entered this licensed market in 1924 as phonograph revenues began to erode under competition from broadcast radio; the VICTOR trademark for radio apparatus was the public-facing declaration of that strategic pivot. RCA's acquisition of Victor in 1929 subsequently collapsed the licensor-licensee relationship, consolidating the radio and phonograph businesses — and both trademark portfolios — under a single corporate entity.

## Significance

This pair captures the moment when Victor Talking Machine Company, then the dominant American phonograph manufacturer, formally entered the radio apparatus market under its established consumer brand. The twenty-eight-day interval between the grant of US1486221A on March 11, 1924, and the VICTOR trademark filing on April 8, 1924, reflects the tempo at which the RCA licensing ecosystem was translating pooled vacuum tube patents into branded commercial products. For the radio-pioneers project, the pair documents a structural feature of early radio commerce: the RCA patent pool created a class of licensed manufacturers whose trademark filings — like Serial No. 71195203 — are the recoverable evidence of that licensing activity in the USPTO record. The RCA-Victor merger of 1929 brought this particular licensor-licensee relationship to its logical end, uniting the underlying patents and the consumer trademark in a single portfolio.
