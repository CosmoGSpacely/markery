## Overview

Leffingwell's report on applying the Taylor system of scientific management to
office work is the most direct contemporary account of the administrative
revolution that produced the demand for card index and filing equipment. Written
at the peak of the scientific management movement, it documents how companies
including manufacturing firms reorganized their clerical operations using the same
analytical methods Taylor applied to factory floors. The companies cited —
Curtis Publishing, Montgomery Ward, Chicago Ferrotype — are exactly the kind of
mid-to-large American organizations that would have been buying Remington Rand and
Library Bureau products in this period.

Directly relevant: the book demonstrates that the demand for systematic filing
was not passive — it was actively manufactured by a management consulting movement
that measured waste in every clerical operation and prescribed systematic indexing
as the cure. The patents and trademarks in this project represent the product side
of that movement; Leffingwell represents the organizational ideology that drove
the sales.

## Relevant passages

### Scientific management applied to office records

> "About ten years ago I began the application to office work of the principles
> of the Taylor system, including planning, analysis, time and motion study, task
> and bonus plans, and the like. Gradually a definite plan for applying this type
> of management to office work was built up, and to the results I obtained this
> report is devoted. Planning in business has always been an essential, but often
> this planning has been confined to the big problems. My experience has been
> that the more the details are planned, the less frequently the big problems
> become sources of anxiety, for usually the big problem is merely a collection
> of detail." (Foreword, p. v)

Context note: Leffingwell positions filing and card indexing as part of the same
scientific revolution that Taylor applied to factory production. This framing
explains why Remington Rand's acquisition of the Rand Kardex Bureau and Library
Bureau properties carried strategic weight: in the early 1920s, card index
equipment was associated with modern scientific management, not mere office
furniture. Owning the leading brand in that category was a position in the
management consulting economy as much as in the office supplies market.

### Vertical filing and the card index decision

> "There are three ways of filing in a vertical file, viz., alphabetical,
> numerical and geographical. Each one has its strong points and to each one, of
> course, some objections can be made. Satisfactory files of each kind are in use
> throughout the country in various institutions, and necessarily there must be
> some reason why these files are giving satisfaction. It is of benefit to know
> the strong points and advantages of each one, in order that after we have
> examined the conditions in any place, we may be able to decide what kind of
> arrangement should be presented for a particular kind of business. The
> alphabetical seems to be the most natural method of filing correspondence."
> (p. 160)

Context note: The three-way choice Leffingwell describes is the exact choice
that SOUNDEX and VARIADEX were designed to resolve for name-based records. The
alphabetical file "seems most natural" but breaks down at scale because of
spelling variants — which is the precise problem that phonetic indexing (SOUNDEX)
and systematic cross-reference (VARIADEX) addressed. Leffingwell's treatment
confirms that the patent-holders were targeting a recognized pain point in
mainstream office management practice.

### Card indexes in manufacturing-company offices

> "It does not require a card index, as the numerical file does, and for this
> reason is cheaper to install. In the alphabetical file reference is made to the
> folders direct instead of first to the card in the index and then to the
> numbered folder, as in the numerical system. All of the correspondence is filed
> under one system. With the numerical file it is necessary to maintain an
> alphabetical file for the transient correspondence, as well as those who do not
> write often enough to warrant giving them a separate folder. So in the numerical
> file it may be necessary to refer first to the index and then, if no card is
> found, to the miscellaneous file." (p. 160)

Context note: This passage documents the overhead cost of card index systems
that the KARDEX visible record design was engineered to eliminate. A visible-tab
card tray lets the clerk scan the entire index at a glance without a separate
lookup step. The engineering problem that produced US patents in this project's
scope was, in contemporary management terms, the reduction of motion waste in
card-index lookup — which Leffingwell measured and priced in the same way Taylor
measured motion waste on factory floors.
