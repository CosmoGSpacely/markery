## The Invention

US1527374A, filed October 27, 1921, and granted February 24, 1925, describes a method of assembling a loose-leaf book in which individual sheets can be added, removed, and rearranged without disturbing the binding mechanism. The assignee is the Wilson Jones Loose Leaf Company, and the inventor is George W. Newman, one of the most prolific engineers in Wilson Jones's patent record. The patent addresses a specific mechanical problem: how to allow loose-leaf sheets to project beyond the edges of the binder so that their tabs or labels remain visible when the book is closed. This projecting-tab feature is what distinguishes a visible-index binder from a standard loose-leaf binder — a clerk can scan a closed binder and read the label on each division without opening it.

## The Mark

VI-DEX (registration 0235764) covered "ring binders, solid-post binders, extensible-post binders, filler sheets for loose-leaf ring binders and post binders, and projecting-tab index sheets for loose-leaf ring binders and post binders." The abbreviation decodes directly: VI = visible index; DEX = index, the suffix adopted widely in the filing-system trade for indexing products. The goods description confirms the reading — "projecting-tab index sheets" are precisely the sheets whose tabs project beyond the binder edge to create the visible-index function. The mark was filed as a stylized wordmark, not plain type.

## The Connection

The link between US1527374A and VI-DEX rests on three converging points: entity (both assigned to Wilson Jones entities), timing (first commercial use of the VI-DEX name is recorded as June 27, 1925 — four months after the patent issued), and goods (the patent describes the projecting-tab mechanism; the trademark covers projecting-tab index sheets). All three converge on the same product: the patent is the mechanism, the mark is the name under which that mechanism reached the market.

## Historical Context

The trademark was not filed until 1927-07, more than two years after the product's commercial introduction. This gap was common in the period — companies often established a mark in commerce before seeking formal protection, registering only once the product had proven itself. By 1927, Wilson Jones was filing several trademarks in rapid succession, a cluster that suggests a deliberate decision to formalize the company's product-name portfolio after a period of commercial growth rather than filing each mark as its product launched.

## Significance

The VI-DEX pair documents Wilson Jones's entry into the visible-index market segment that Remington Rand's Kardex system had helped define. Where Kardex used overlapping trays, VI-DEX used projecting tabs in a loose-leaf binder — a different mechanical approach to the same business problem: making large indexes scannable at a glance. That competing firms were filing patents and trademarks for visible-index products in the same window (1925–1930) reflects genuine competition within the office-systems market, and the filing record is the evidence of it.
