# SHANNON: An Acquired Name in the American Filing Market

The SHANNON trademark (serial 71295144, filed January 23, 1930) was registered by Yawman and Erbe Mfg. Co. of Rochester, New York, covering arch-type binders, arch files, perforators, file guides, and related filing equipment. The filing came 51 days after Yawman & Erbe was granted US1738120A, "File Folder," on December 3, 1929. The SHANNON mark documents a pattern distinct from the other pairs in this project: not a newly invented product being trademarked, but an established brand name being registered in the American market by a company that had absorbed it.

---

## The Patent

US1738120A, titled "File Folder" and granted December 3, 1929, was assigned to Yawman & Erbe Mfg Co. The patent covers improvements to the file folder — the basic unit of the vertical filing system. Yawman & Erbe held an extensive filing-appliance patent portfolio stretching back to 1902; this patent sits within their mature filing-systems line rather than representing a technical breakthrough.

The applicant date precedes the grant by several years, indicating the innovation was in development well before the trademark was filed. The connection between a file-folder patent and an arch-binder trademark is not one of direct claim correspondence — US1738120A describes folders for vertical files, while SHANNON covered arch-type binders (a different binding mechanism). The pairing documents Yawman & Erbe's simultaneous work across multiple filing-system categories: vertical files and arch binders were distinct product lines serving overlapping markets, and the company held patents and trademarks in both.

---

## The Trademark

SHANNON (registration 0272881) covered a broad goods description: *"arch-type binders and file boards, arch files, hand-operated perforators, file compressors and covers for arch-type binders, indexes for arch binders, file guides, transfer cases, and loose-leaf binders."* No first-use date is recorded in the case file. The mark eventually expired (status 900).

The SHANNON name did not originate with Yawman & Erbe. Shannon Brothers Ltd. of London had been manufacturing the Shannon lever-arch file since the 1880s — a distinctive arch-mechanism binder in which a metal arch rod holds perforated papers, secured by a lever-operated clasp. The design was patented in Britain and widely adopted in European offices before reaching the American market. By 1930, when Yawman & Erbe filed the American trademark, SHANNON was already a recognized name for arch-mechanism filing in the trade. Yawman & Erbe's filing registered that name in the United States under their ownership, either through a licensing arrangement or an acquisition of American rights.

---

## The Correspondence

The 51-day gap between US1738120A (December 3, 1929) and the SHANNON trademark filing (January 23, 1930) is consistent with a coordinated filing strategy, though the technical correspondence between the patent and the goods is by product category rather than by direct invention. Yawman & Erbe was a filing-equipment company; the patent covers a filing-equipment improvement; the trademark covers filing equipment. The temporal proximity suggests the company was actively securing its intellectual property portfolio in late 1929 and early 1930, bringing both a new patent and a major brand name into formal registration within the same two-month window.

The SHANNON pair requires a distinction the other pairs in this project do not: the trademark registers a brand name whose underlying technical concept was not invented by the trademark owner. Yawman & Erbe were adapting an established product category to their manufacturing capabilities, not commercializing their own invention. The filing record documents that adaptation — specifically, the moment the American rights to the SHANNON name were formalized in the USPTO record.

---

## Historical Significance

SHANNON is still manufactured today. Lever-arch files sold under the SHANNON name are available in stationary markets worldwide, making this the only confirmed pair in the information-systems project whose commercial lineage is unbroken from the early twentieth century to the present. The Yawman & Erbe trademark registration of January 1930 is one link in that chain — the moment an American manufacturer formalized its claim to a name that had originated in a London workshop forty years earlier and would outlast the company that registered it by nearly a century.
