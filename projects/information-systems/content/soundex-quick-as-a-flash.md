# SOUNDEX QUICK AS A FLASH: The Advertising Mark

The SOUNDEX QUICK AS A FLASH trademark (serial 71255821, filed October 8, 1927) is the second of two marks Rand Kardex Bureau, Inc. registered for its phonetic indexing system in 1927, and the one that rests directly on Margaret K. Odell's improvement patent (US1435663A, granted November 14, 1922). Where the first SOUNDEX mark presented a clean institutional wordmark, this one offered a lightning bolt.

---

## The Patent

US1435663A, titled "Index" and granted November 14, 1922, was filed by Margaret K. Odell on December 19, 1921, and assigned to the Remington Typewriter Company. Odell's patent built on Robert C. Russell's foundational Soundex patent (US1261167A, 1918), extending the phonetic coding method for surname indexing. Both patents shared the same assignee and the same commercial purpose: enabling clerks to locate records by approximate sound rather than exact spelling.

The Odell patent's title — simply "Index" — understates what it describes. The system reduced surnames to a one-letter, three-digit code based on consonant sounds, eliminating vowels and collapsing phonetically equivalent pairs. The practical result was a filing system that handled spelling variation systematically, particularly useful for records involving immigrant names transcribed by ear. Russell's patent established the method; Odell's refined it. Together they formed the technical foundation of the SOUNDEX product line.

---

## The Trademark

SOUNDEX QUICK AS A FLASH (registration 0247084) covered identical goods to the first SOUNDEX mark: *"blank and partially-printed cards and forms for indexes, indexes consisting of blank or partially-printed forms or cards, index guide cards and separator cards of the type used for phonetic indexes, and frames and holders for index cards."* The first-use date recorded is May 31, 1927 — more than four months before the October filing, and roughly concurrent with the final stages of the Remington Rand merger.

The mark itself is visually unlike the sober SOUNDEX wordmark filed seven months earlier. The TSDR record notes its draw code as 3000 — a design mark — with the registered design featuring bold script lettering bisected by a large lightning bolt, with the slogan "quick as a Flash" in cursive below. The visual vocabulary is unmistakably of its moment: speed, electricity, modernity. The same idiom appeared in radio advertisements and automobile promotions of the late 1920s. A filing system sold on efficiency needed to make efficiency feel exciting.

---

## The Correspondence

The SOUNDEX QUICK AS A FLASH mark was filed seven months after SOUNDEX, covering identical goods under the same assignee lineage. The Odell patent (US1435663A) had been assigned to Remington Typewriter Company since 1921; by 1927, both the Russell and Odell patents had passed to the Rand Kardex Bureau as part of the corporate consolidation that would become Remington Rand. Filing the second mark in October 1927 — after the merger was announced and before it was finalized — suggests Rand Kardex was securing the commercial identity of its phonetic index product line from multiple angles: a technical wordmark for purchasing agents, a bold design mark for broader advertising.

The first-use date of May 31, 1927 is instructive. That is the date Rand Kardex Bureau was actively selling under the slogan, not just testing it. By October 1927, the slogan had been in commercial use for nearly five months and warranted its own formal registration.

---

## Historical Significance

The two SOUNDEX marks together document a deliberate dual-brand strategy: one mark establishes the technical product name (SOUNDEX), the other registers the advertising identity (SOUNDEX QUICK AS A FLASH). This practice — filing a family of marks around a single product — would become standard in twentieth-century brand management, but it was still relatively unusual in the late 1920s for a specialist business-equipment manufacturer. Rand Kardex Bureau's decision to register both suggests a company that understood its phonetic index system was a significant commercial asset worth protecting on multiple registers simultaneously.
