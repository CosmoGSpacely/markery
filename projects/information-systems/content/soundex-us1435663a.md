## The Invention

US1435663A, titled "Index" and granted November 14, 1922, was filed by Margaret K. Odell on December 19, 1921, and assigned to the Remington Typewriter Company. Odell's patent refined the phonetic surname-coding method that underlies the Soundex system: a surname is reduced to a one-letter, three-digit code based on consonant sounds, discarding vowels and collapsing phonetically equivalent consonants. *Smith*, *Smyth*, and *Smythe* all encode to **S530**. The result is a filing system in which a clerk can locate a record without knowing the exact spelling of a name — only its approximate sound. The Odell patent extended the earlier foundational Soundex filing (US1261167A, 1918), and the two together formed the technical basis of the SOUNDEX product line.

## The Mark

SOUNDEX (registration 0230958) is the institutional wordmark for Rand Kardex Bureau's phonetic-index product line — a straightforward italic serif that reads as precision business equipment rather than consumer goods. It covered "blank and partially-printed cards and forms for indexes, indexes consisting of blank or partially-printed forms or cards, index guide cards and separator cards of the type used for phonetic indexes, and frames and holders for index cards." The first-use date — August 10, 1926 — establishes that Rand Kardex was selling phonetic-index products eight months before filing the trademark on 1927-03.

## The Connection

The SOUNDEX mark named the commercial product built directly on the Odell phonetic-coding patent. The goods description — cards and guide cards "of the type used for phonetic indexes" — ties the mark explicitly to the coding method US1435663A describes. The patent had been Remington property since 1922; the mark was filed by Rand Kardex Bureau in 1927, the moment the algorithm's patent-holder and the filing-system manufacturer were consolidating into a single corporate entity.

## Historical Context

The timing of the filing is not incidental. In 1927 — the year the SOUNDEX mark was filed — the Rand Corporation merged with the Remington Typewriter Company to form Remington Rand, Inc. The Soundex patents had been Remington's property since 1918 and 1922; the Rand Kardex Bureau filing represents the moment the patent-holder and the manufacturer were becoming the same company. Rand Kardex was, in effect, trademarking a product line built on patents it was about to inherit through merger. The commercial logic was clear: American offices in the 1920s managed vast quantities of surname-indexed records, much of it transcribed by ear, and a phonetic index collapsed endemic spelling variation into a single lookup.

## Significance

The most consequential deployment of the Soundex algorithm came not from Rand Kardex but from the federal government: beginning in the late 1930s, the Works Progress Administration employed workers to create Soundex indexes for the U.S. Census records supporting Social Security age and citizenship verification. Those phonetically coded, hand-filed index cards became one of the most heavily used genealogical resources in American history. A researcher today navigating a census Soundex index is using a system whose commercial form was registered by Rand Kardex Bureau in March 1927 — the pairing this record documents.
