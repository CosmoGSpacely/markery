Being a Complete and Faithful Record

A Most Singular Account of the Patent Data Expedition,
Comprising the Attempts, Reversals, and Partial Redemption
Attending the Collection of Historical Patent Records
for the Years 1900–1939

Composed in the Year 2026  ·  Markery Research Files
Prologue

In Which the Nature of the Commission is Explained, and the Investigator Sets Forth with Every Confidence

The proposition, on its face, was not unreasonable. Indeed, to a mind unacquainted with the peculiar administrative geography of the United States Patent and Trademark Office as it stood in the spring of the year 1926—that is to say, of 2026—it might have seemed positively straightforward. The markery project, a most admirable undertaking in the study of American commercial history through the medium of trademark and patent records, required that a database be constructed. This database, to be designated patents.duckdb, would contain the records of some several thousand patents granted between the years 1900 and 1939, touching upon the subject of pre-computer information systems: the filing cabinets, index cards, visible record files, tabulating machines, and phonetic coding schemes by which the American commercial enterprise organised its knowledge before the age of the digital computer.

The plan was simple. The tool was identified. The API was known. One had only to knock upon the door, state one's requirements in the proper form, and receive the data in good order. The script was written. The endpoint was hard-coded. The rate limiter was set at a gentlemanly 0.25 seconds between requests, so as not to impose unduly upon one's host.

Reader, the door was not there.

Chapter the First

In Which the Investigator Makes His First Approach and Receives From the Universe a Most Succinct Reply

The PatentsView API, administered by the United States Patent and Trademark Office in partnership with several august academic institutions, had for many years provided an estimable service to researchers of patent history. Its address—search.patentsview.org—was noted in the official documentation. It appeared in dozens of scholarly papers. It was, by all accounts, a serviceable and well-maintained establishment.

The investigator's first act was therefore perfectly orthodox: a request was sent to the Domain Name System, that great postal directory of the Internet, inquiring after the address of search.patentsview.org. The reply arrived with the terrible swiftness of a magistrate pronouncing sentence upon a man who had waited in the dock for six years:

From the Domain Name System, with all due dispatch: ** server can't find search.patentsview.org: NXDOMAIN

NXDOMAIN. Non-Existent Domain. A brisk, bureaucratic formulation meaning, in the common tongue: there is no such place. Not "temporarily unavailable." Not "please try again later." Simply: it does not exist. It never existed, so far as the present state of affairs is concerned. Whatever was here before has been removed, as a name is struck from the register of the living.

The investigator, unwilling to accept so curt a dismissal, applied to two additional registries—those maintained by the firms of Google and Cloudflare, both of which enjoy reputations for thoroughness. Their replies were identical. The domain was, by any and every account, gone.

Verdict
search.patentsview.org — NXDOMAIN on Google DNS (8.8.8.8), Cloudflare DNS (1.1.1.1), and the system resolver alike. The domain does not exist. The server, if it ever ran, ran no longer.

Chapter the Second

In Which the Cause of the Disappearance is Revealed, and Is Found to Be Official in Nature

A mystery demands an explanation, and the investigator, being of a methodical disposition, went in search of one. A web search revealed the truth with a promptness that, in different circumstances, might have been refreshing: on the twentieth day of March, in the year 2026, the United States Patent and Trademark Office had completed a migration.

This migration—and here one pauses to savour the word, which in the context of government computing carries approximately the same comfort as a surgeon's assurance that the procedure is "perfectly routine"—had transferred the functions of the PatentsView platform to the USPTO's new Open Data Portal, located at data.uspto.gov. The old site had been decommissioned. Its domain had been struck from the DNS. Its API had been retired. Its documentation now redirected to a transition guide.

The transition guide itself directed interested parties to data@uspto.gov.

The transition guide did not provide a new API endpoint.

This is the manner in which great bureaucracies move: not with a single dramatic gesture, but with a slow, patient redistribution of inconvenience, until the researcher who sought one thing in one place finds himself reading an FAQ about something else entirely in a slightly different place, the original thing having been quietly absorbed into the institutional substrate like a coin dropped into a sofa.

There was also, it emerged during this investigation, a detail of some consequence that had been overlooked in the original plan: the PatentsView database—the very database whose API had been so confidently invoked—covered US patent records from 1976 to the present. Its historical records, its supposedly comprehensive catalogue of the American inventive tradition, extended no further back than the administration of Gerald Ford. The patents of 1900–1939, the filing cabinets and index cards and punched-card tabulators, the entire subject of the present inquiry: never in the database at all.

PatentsView data coverage (per official documentation): Granted patents: 1976 – present Pre-grant publications: 2001 – present Historical record (1900–1939): not included

Chapter the Third

Being an Account of the Hall of Many Doors, in Which Every Corridor Leads to the Same Room

The new establishment—data.uspto.gov—was large, well-appointed, and possessed of a front door that opened with great readiness. Every enquiry received, in due course, an HTTP response of 200 OK. One cannot fault the hospitality. The difficulty was of a subtler kind.

The investigator, reasoning that the new portal must surely contain the successor to the old API, began constructing requests. He tried the path one would expect for a patent query. He tried several variations. He tried the path suggested by the old PatentsView API documentation, modified for the new domain. He tried paths that seemed, on the face of it, to be precisely the sort of paths a patent API would inhabit. Each request returned, without exception, an HTTP 200 response—indicating success—and a body of precisely 20,666 bytes of HTML.

The same 20,666 bytes. Every time. A JavaScript single-page application. The lobby of the building, returned in lieu of whatever room one had actually requested. The USPTO's Open Data Portal, like a magician's cabinet, contained only one thing no matter which panel one opened: itself.

Sample exchange (repeated, with variations, some dozen times): GET https://data.uspto.gov/api/v1/patent/?q={"patent_id":"1261167"}&per_page=1 HTTP/2 200 content-type: text/html content-length: 20666 <!doctype html><html lang="en"><head>... [20,621 bytes of JavaScript application follow]

The portal was, in the terminology of modern web development, a single-page application: a structure in which the server presents one HTML document containing JavaScript code, and that JavaScript code, when executed in a web browser, renders the actual content by making further API calls to a backend that is not publicly documented. It is an admirably modern approach to the distribution of information, provided one is a web browser. For a Python script carrying a research query about index card patents from 1918, it offers nothing whatsoever.

Verdict
data.uspto.gov — Accessible, returns HTTP 200 for all paths, delivers 20,666 bytes of a JavaScript single-page application in response to structured JSON patent queries. Also: covers only applications filed from 2001 onward. Useless for 1900–1939 research by both nature and design.

Chapter the Fourth

The Dead Domains, and the Address That Existed But Could Not Be Reached — Being the Most Remarkable Episode of the Entire Affair

The investigator, a creature of systematic habit, now turned to the remaining USPTO domains. Bulkdata.uspto.gov, from which the weekly patent grant files had once been distributed: the DNS returned a blank. Not NXDOMAIN, precisely—the zone existed—but no record. No answer. The equivalent of a street where the houses have been numbered but none yet built.

Then came the moment which, of all the episodes in this singular chronicle, most recommends itself to the notice of those who study the relationship between official documentation and operational reality. The investigator, consulting his notes, found mention of a system called PatFT—the Patent Full-Text Search database—which, according to the USPTO's own historical materials, contained the full text of every United States patent granted since the year of Our Lord 1790. Seventeen hundred and ninety. This was precisely the scope required. This was, in point of fact, exactly the thing.

A DNS query was dispatched.

The DNS replied.

The reply contained an address.

DNS resolution of patft.uspto.gov via Google DNS (8.8.8.8): Non-authoritative answer: Name: patft.uspto.gov Address: 10.10.10.10

The reader who is familiar with the conventions of computer networking will immediately apprehend the situation. The range 10.0.0.0/8—of which 10.10.10.10 is an entirely typical inhabitant—is a private address space, reserved for use within internal networks. It is, in the geography of the Internet, roughly equivalent to a room number in a building to which the public has no access. The address 10.10.10.10 is not located anywhere one can reach from the public internet. It is located in the USPTO's own internal network, reachable only by USPTO staff, from USPTO computers, within the USPTO's walls.

The Patent Full-Text Search system—the system containing every patent since 1790, the very system the investigator required—was still running. Its DNS entry was intact. It had an address. That address was, with the greatest possible respect to everyone involved, in a room to which the public is not admitted, has never been admitted, and will not be admitted while the present arrangement continues.

The investigator stared at the address 10.10.10.10 for what felt, to an outside observer, like a measurable period of time.

Verdict
patft.uspto.gov — Resolves to 10.10.10.10. Private address space. USPTO internal network only. Contains every US patent since 1790. Inaccessible from the public internet. Cannot be reached by this or any other method available to the civilian researcher.

Chapter the Fifth

Fixed Response Content — the Last Gasp of the Patent Public Search System

One domain remained: ppubs.uspto.gov, the Patent Public Search, the modern successor to PatFT, the system by which members of the public might, according to the documentation, query the full patent record from their own computers without requiring access to an internal network resolving to 10.10.10.10. The front page returned HTTP 200. The HTML was 1,456 bytes. Modest, but a response is a response.

The investigator, having located the source code of a Python library (patent-client) that was designed to interact with this very system, followed its logic precisely: first, retrieve the main page; second, POST a request to establish a session; third, submit a search query to the documented endpoint. The library's own code named the session URL as ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/users/me/session.

The session endpoint returned:

Response from ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/users/me/session: HTTP/2 404 Fixed response content

"Fixed response content." Not an error message. Not a deprecation notice. Not a redirect to a transition guide. Simply: Fixed response content. A phrase of such magnificent vacuity that one could stare at it for some time without gaining any information whatsoever. It communicates neither what the fixed content is, nor why a response has been fixed, nor for whom, nor toward what purpose. It is, in its way, a masterpiece of the administrative sublime: the response that says everything by saying nothing, and in so doing, says nothing.

Every endpoint beneath ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/ returned the same. The search endpoint: fixed. The document endpoint: fixed. The session endpoint: fixed. The Patent Public Search had been decommissioned—like its predecessors, like its neighbours, like everything within reach—as part of the Great Migration of March 2026.

Verdict
ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/ — Decommissioned. All endpoints return HTTP 404 "Fixed response content." No search functionality available. Decommissioned as part of March 2026 USPTO migration to data.uspto.gov.

Chapter the Sixth

In Which the Investigator Attempts the Mountain of Gold, and Finds It Defended by Mechanisms Both Ingenious and Implacable

Google Patents, that great repository of the world's inventive output, contains every US patent from the year 1790 to the present day, fully indexed, searchable, and presented with commendable clarity. Its URL structure is well-known. The page for United States Patent No. 1,261,167—Robert C. Russell's Soundex indexing system of 1918—loads in the ordinary web browser without ceremony or obstruction. The PDF of the patent is freely downloadable. The investigator had, in earlier sessions, obtained PDFs of several patents by exactly this route.

The difficulty was one of scale. Individual lookups were tolerated. Bulk queries were not.

Examination of the browser's network traffic revealed an internal API: patents.google.com/xhr/query, which accepted search parameters and returned structured JSON including title, filing date, grant date, assignee name, CPC classification codes, and patent number—precisely the fields required for patents.duckdb. A simple keyword query returned results. The investigator's spirits, which had by now assumed a posture of professional resignation, briefly rallied.

They were not permitted to rally for long. A query incorporating a date filter returned HTTP 503. A query with an assignee: operator returned HTTP 503. A second attempt at a simple query returned HTTP 503. Google, whose automated defences are both comprehensive and unsentimental, had observed the investigator's pattern of requests, formed its own conclusions, and ceased to participate in the exercise.

Response from patents.google.com, after several queries: HTTP/1.1 503 <title>Sorry...</title> <!-- 503 means: we have identified you as a robot, which is accurate, and have nothing further to add -->
Verdict
Google Patents / XHR API — Contains the required data. Accessible for individual lookups and occasional searches. Blocks structured bulk queries with HTTP 503. Rate-limits aggressively after a small number of requests. Terms of service prohibit automated scraping. Not a viable bulk data source.

Chapter the Seventh

The Toll-Gate, or, How One Comes to Understand the Phrase "Free for Non-Commercial Use"

The Lens—lens.org—presents itself as a public good, a free and open repository of all the world's patent and scholarly literature, "unsurveilled" and "without advertising." It covers the full US patent record from 1790 to the present. It supports structured queries by CPC classification and date range. Its API—api.lens.org/patent/search—responds to a test request with a prompt and well-formatted 401 Unauthorised, indicating that the server is operational and merely awaiting proper credentials. One test, and hope returns.

The API documentation is clear. Access requires an API token. Tokens are obtained from one's user profile, under the API & Data tab. There is a "Trial" plan and a "Custom Access" plan. "Trial" is described as "free for non-commercial or limited academic use."

The word "Trial" does considerable work in that sentence.

The trial period is fourteen days. After fourteen days, the token expires. Custom Access—the plan required for ongoing research use—is priced, depending on the level of access required, at between one thousand and five thousand United States dollars per year.

The "free" academic workspace, one discovers upon closer inspection, applies to the web interface. The API—the programmatic access required for building a database—is, after the fourteen-day courtesy period, a commercial product. The Lens is admirably candid about this. The researcher who had hoped for a free and open API for long-term research use must, at this point, set aside hope and consult a budget.

Verdict
The Lens API — Server reachable, API functional, covers 1790–present with CPC/USPC classification queries. Free tier: 14-day trial only. Ongoing access: $1,000–$5,000/year. Not suitable for a self-funded historical research project without institutional backing.

Chapter the Eighth

The Justia Episode, the NBER Revelation, the Internet Archive, and Other Brief Appearances by Sources of No Assistance

For completeness, the record should note the following additional enquiries and their outcomes.

Justia Patents, a legal information service maintaining a searchable archive of US patents: HTTP 403 Forbidden, accompanied by a formidable array of client-hint headers suggesting that the site's automated defences had made their assessment before the investigator had finished typing the URL.

The NBER Patent Data File, a research dataset assembled by the National Bureau of Economic Research: an admirably thorough compilation covering United States utility patents from 1963 to 1999. The investigator, having spent considerable effort confirming the 1963 start date, found himself unable to explain to the database that he required the forty years before that date.

The Internet Archive, that great institution of digital preservation: a search for archived USPTO patent grant files from the year 1918 returned zero results. The Archive, which has preserved the Geocities website of a fourteen-year-old from 2001 in considerable detail, had not thought to preserve the structural data of the American patent system for the years 1900–1939.

The Google Books / USPTO Patent Text Data, a project in which Google scanned and digitised all US patent documents: bulk text files available for download, beginning with the year 1976. The year 1976. Once more, 1976. It seems that the year 1976 represents, in the imagination of every organisation that has ever compiled a patent database, the natural starting point of human ingenuity. What occurred before 1976—the filing cabinets, the index cards, the punched card tabulators, the phonetic indexing systems, the entire subject of the present inquiry—apparently took place in the oral tradition.

The EPA Espacenet OPS API—the European Patent Office's Open Patent Services—was attempted unauthenticated and returned, with some justice, an HTTP 403 Fair Use Policy Violation. Authenticated access requires a free registration. The registration was submitted.

Chapter the Ninth and Last

The One Unlocked Door — In Which a Single Resource Presents Itself as Both Available and, for the Moment, Pending

The European Patent Office's Open Patent Services API—maintained at ops.epo.org and accessible to registered users without charge, subject to a fair-use limit of four gigabytes per month—covers the full historical patent record of the United States from 1790 to the present day. It supports queries by date range and by CPC classification. It returns structured data including assignee names, inventor names, abstracts, and classification codes. It is, in every respect, the resource that the investigation required.

The registration process is free. It requires the creation of an account at developers.epo.org, the definition of an application, and the receipt of a Consumer Key and Consumer Key Secret by which OAuth 2.0 authentication tokens may be obtained. The maximum result set per query is two thousand records—a constraint that can be managed by subdividing requests by year or subclass—but is otherwise undemanding.

The registration was submitted. It was received. It was, as of the conclusion of this chronicle, under review.

The single unlocked door in the entire edifice—the one door behind which the required data sat in good order, awaiting a proper enquiry—was unlocked, and open, and attended by an official who had received the investigator's card and was prepared to admit him at such time as the review of his credentials had been completed. Which was not, at present, now.

Moral & Conclusion

The patents themselves—the Russell Soundex patent of 1918, the Odell improvement of 1922, the filing systems and index cards and visible record devices and tabulating machines of the great age of paper-based information management—sit undisturbed in their various archives, waiting with the patience that only paper can achieve. They were there before PatentsView was conceived, and they remain after it has been dissolved into the general substrate of data.uspto.gov. The records of the American Patent Office are not lost. They are merely, at present, inconveniently arranged.

The lesson of this expedition is perhaps the lesson of all historical research: that the distance between a thing's existence and one's ability to reach it is precisely the space in which the historian must work, and in which the historian earns, if anything is earned, whatever small claim to usefulness the historical enterprise can make. The index cards of 1918 were designed to organise information that was difficult to retrieve. It is, at minimum, appropriate that the records of their invention should present similar difficulties.

The EPO registration is under review. When it clears, the work will continue.
