## Thesis

American office-systems manufacturers of the 1900–1939 period systematically bridged the gap between mechanical invention and commercial product by filing trademarks for the branded names under which their patented indexing, filing, and record-management systems were sold. The information-systems project documents this correspondence: for each confirmed pair, a specific patent describes the technical mechanism; the companion trademark registers the name under which that mechanism reached the market. Together they reveal how a generation of manufacturers — Remington Rand, Wilson Jones, Yawman & Erbe — translated mechanical innovation into branded consumer products, establishing the vocabulary of the modern office long before digital systems arrived.

The deeper argument is methodological: the patent-trademark correspondence, reconstructed from the filing record rather than from trade press or corporate archives, is itself a historically productive research technique. Where physical corporate archives have been destroyed and trade catalogs are fragmentary, the legal record survives. SOUNDEX, KARDEX, VI-DEX, and REDIREF can be reconstructed as products — not just as names — because the filing record preserved both the technical description and the commercial claim simultaneously.

## Scope Boundaries

**In scope:**
- USPTO trademark filings 1900–1939 (the bulk dataset boundary)
- US patents in CPC classes B42F, B42D, B41J, B41L, G06C, G06K, G09F — the mechanical information management industry
- Confirmed entity portfolio companies: Remington Rand (including predecessors Remington Typewriter, Rand Kardex Bureau, Kardex Rand), Wilson Jones, Yawman & Erbe, Boorum & Pease
- Pre-1900 patents reached via citation chaining from confirmed patents (Phase 6D)
- Post-1939 trademark filings by confirmed entities showing commercial continuity through the 1940s–1950s (Phase 6D)

**Out of scope:**
- International trademark systems except where a US filing documents a British predecessor mark (SHANNON note)
- Post-WWII product lines not directly continuous with 1900–1939 patents
- Computing and early electronic data processing — the project ends where electromechanical tabulation begins (the Hollerith boundary)
- Legal analysis: nothing in this project constitutes trademark clearance or availability opinion

**The Hollerith boundary:** Herman Hollerith's tabulating machine work intersects this period but is treated as adjacent, not central — his patents are in different CPC classes and his company's commercial products are tabulating machines, not card filing or visible-index systems in the sense this project documents. Austrian's biography is reference material for context, not a primary source for confirmed pairs.

## Target Audience

Layered:

1. **General reader** — landing page, thematic essays, and entity summaries are written for any reader who can follow a historical argument grounded in primary sources. No database terminology. Define USPTO, CPC, TSDR on first use. Explain what a trademark serial number is before citing one.

2. **Specialist reader** — match essays and source notes are written to scholarly standards: serial numbers and publication numbers cited explicitly, first-use dates treated as self-reported evidence, file-destroyed gaps acknowledged. A patent historian or trademark researcher should find these pages useful as reference.

3. **Wikipedia standard** — any content drafted for Wikipedia contribution must meet encyclopedic register: neutral point of view, secondary-source grounding, no original research claims that are not directly supported by cited sources. The SOUNDEX enrichment (adding primary-source citations to the existing article) and the KARDEX draft article are the two Wikipedia targets.
