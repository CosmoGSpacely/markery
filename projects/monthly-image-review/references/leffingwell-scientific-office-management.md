## Overview

Leffingwell documents the application of Taylor's scientific management to office
work in American manufacturing companies. For this project, the most relevant
dimension is the book's treatment of how manufacturing-company offices managed
their commercial records — the same type of organization that filed the marks
appearing in this gallery. Companies like Chicago Pneumatic Tool Company,
operating in the same industrial economy Leffingwell describes, faced exactly the
administrative record-keeping pressures his book analyzes.

The book is also relevant as evidence of the management-consulting ecosystem that
surrounded the office equipment industry in the 1910s and 1920s. Leffingwell's
clients were manufacturing companies reorganizing their clerical operations; those
same companies were the market for the filing equipment and card index systems
whose patents and trademarks appear in the information-systems project. The
demand side and the product side of that market are documented, respectively, by
this book and the primary records in this project.

## Relevant passages

### Manufacturing companies as the primary clients

> "For thirty-five years the late Frederick W. Taylor devoted all his genius and
> all his energy to the development of that type of factory management which came
> to be known as the Taylor system of scientific management. For more than twenty
> years he was continuously engaged in applying his methods to various kinds of
> manufacturing establishments. About ten years ago I began the application to
> office work of the principles of the Taylor system, including planning,
> analysis, time and motion study, task and bonus plans, and the like." (Foreword,
> p. v)

Context note: Leffingwell explicitly situates his work as the office extension of
factory scientific management. The manufacturing companies in this gallery — CP,
Wilson Jones, others in the industrial tool and office equipment sector — were the
target audience for this approach. Their trademark filings in the 1910s–1930s
coincide with the peak of the scientific management movement. Understanding the
marks in context means understanding the organizations that made them, and
Leffingwell provides the clearest contemporary account of how those organizations
thought about administrative efficiency.

### Systematic card-record keeping in manufacturing offices

> "The stockkeeper has a card index to the stock, classified and cross-indexed
> under the following items: As to form number / As to bin number / As to name /
> As to department / As to use. This extensive cross-indexing has only to be done
> once and saves an immense amount of time when a new clerk has to look up stock.
> As a rule, however, since the bin number is on the balance-of-classified-stores
> record, this number is quoted on the requisition and saves all references to the
> index by the storekeeper." (p. 58)

Context note: The card-index cross-referencing practice Leffingwell describes in
a manufacturing stock-room context is the same infrastructure that was applied to
trademark and patent records. Chicago Pneumatic, as a manufacturer of pneumatic
tools and equipment with multiple product lines, would have maintained similar
card records for its commercial properties — including trademark registrations
organized by product line, filing date, and registration status. The visible-tab
card systems that appear in this project's trademark image gallery (many marks
depicting filing and index systems) were the commercial products answering exactly
this administrative need.
