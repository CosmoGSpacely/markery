## The Invention

US1419306A covers an indicating surface gauge — a layout and inspection instrument that holds an adjustable indicating element against a reference surface so that a measured height or position can be set, locked, and transferred to a workpiece. As the title states, the device belongs to the family of surface gauges used in metal-working layout, where repeatable positioning against a flat datum is essential. The patent is classified in CPC G01B (measuring of linear dimensions), consistent with a precision dimensional instrument rather than a cutting or holding tool.

## The Mark

Serial 71185153 is a *figurative* mark — a design device with no word element — registered by The L.S. Starrett Company in 1923 for a broad line of hand tools: brad sets, center punches, clamps, countersinks, cut nippers, drill blocks, hack saws and frames, nail sets, pin vises, pliers, saws, scrapers, screw drivers, scribers, tap wrenches, vises, and wrenches. The registered goods are cutting, holding, and driving tools; the registration does not enumerate measuring instruments. The mark's USPTO design codes (170701, 170708) fall in the "horological, measuring, and controlling instruments" category, so the *imagery* of the device evokes measurement even though the *goods* are hand tools.

## The Connection

This pairing joins one company's patent and one company's mark from the same brief window: both belong to The L.S. Starrett Company, the patent granted June 1922 and the mark filed August 1923. The link is **ownership and era**, not a documented product-to-patent embodiment. The surface gauge of US1419306A is a measuring instrument, and it is *not* among the hand tools enumerated in the mark's goods; nothing in these two records shows that this specific patent was practiced under this specific mark. What the pair does illustrate is that, in the same early-1920s window, Starrett was simultaneously patenting precision measuring instruments and registering brand protection across its wider tool catalog — two facets of one precision-tool identity.

**Editorial note.** The free model that drafted this card asserted that the surface gauge "provides the fundamental measuring principle that underlies many of the listed tools, particularly the drill blocks and pin vises" and "directly supports the functional performance of the trademarked product family." ## Historical Context

In the early 1920s, American machine shops were standardizing around interchangeable parts and tighter tolerances, and L.S. Starrett — founded in Athol, Massachusetts, in 1880 — was among the firms that built a reputation on precision measuring instruments. The surface gauge patent and the catalog-wide trademark filing both date from this expansion, when established toolmakers protected incremental mechanical improvements by patent while registering brand marks to cover their full product lines against imitators.

## Significance

For this project, the pair is a clean example of an **owner-and-era correspondence** rather than a product-patent embodiment: a single firm's measuring-instrument patent and its brand mark, dated within about a year of each other, both rooted in Starrett's precision-tool business. It is a useful counterpoint to tighter goods-matched pairs — the records are co-owned and contemporaneous, but the honest reading stops short of claiming the patent was sold under the mark.
