## The Invention

US1384105A covers a fine-adjustment bevel-protractor — an angle-measuring and layout instrument with a movable limb that can be set to a precise angle and locked, "and the like" extending the claim to related adjustable protractor forms. As an angular measuring device, it belongs to the precision-instrument family and is classified in CPC G01B (measuring of linear and angular dimensions). Like the surface gauge and steel rule paired with the same mark, it is a measuring instrument rather than one of the cutting or holding hand tools the trademark enumerates.

## The Mark

Serial 71185153 is a *figurative* mark — a design device with no word element — registered by The L.S. Starrett Company in 1923 for a broad hand-tool line: brad sets, center punches, clamps, countersinks, cut nippers, drill blocks, hack saws and frames, nail sets, pin vises, pliers, saws, scrapers, screw drivers, scribers, tap wrenches, vises, and wrenches. The registered goods are cutting, holding, and driving tools and do not include protractors or other measuring instruments. The mark's USPTO design codes (170701, 170708) fall in the "measuring and controlling instruments" category, so the device's imagery alludes to measurement while its goods do not.

## The Connection

The bevel-protractor patent (granted July 1921) and the figurative mark (filed August 1923) share a single owner, The L.S. Starrett Company, and a common early-1920s window — a gap of about two years, the widest of the three Starrett pairs here. The correspondence is **owner-and-era**, not a documented embodiment: the protractor is a measuring instrument and is *not* among the mark's enumerated hand tools, and nothing in these records ties this patent to this mark commercially. The pair belongs to the same broad story as the others — Starrett patenting precision measuring instruments while protecting its general tool brand in the same period.

**Editorial note.** The free-model draft asserted that the bevel-protractor "provides the fundamental measuring principle that underlies many of the listed tools, particularly those used for layout and positioning such as center punches and drill blocks," and again invented "Class 6" and "Class 8" attributions absent from the record. Both were removed in human review: the protractor is not among the mark's goods, the listed hand tools do not depend on it, and the class numbers are unsupported. The validator certifies the facts; the interpretive claims required the human gate.

## Historical Context

By the early 1920s the L.S. Starrett Company (founded 1880) was an established American precision-instrument maker. The bevel-protractor patent and the catalog-wide trademark both fall in the interwar standardization of machine-shop work, when toolmakers paired patent protection for mechanical refinements with broad trademark registration to defend their product lines.

## Significance

With the largest date gap of the three pairs, this case is the clearest reminder that co-ownership plus rough contemporaneity is not the same as a product-patent link. It earns its place in the project as an owner-and-era correspondence within Starrett's precision-tool catalog — and as a demonstration that the honest reading depends on the goods record, not on the suggestive alignment of dates.
