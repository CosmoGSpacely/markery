The Invention

US1438617A covers a steel rule combined with a holder, so that the rule can be secured to or against a workpiece while a measurement is read or transferred. As the title indicates, the invention is a graduated measuring rule and its retaining fixture — a dimensional layout instrument. It is classified in CPC G01B (measuring of linear dimensions), placing it squarely among precision measuring devices rather than the cutting and holding tools that fill the associated trademark's goods list.

The Mark

Serial 71185153 is a *figurative* mark — a design device with no word element — registered by The L.S. Starrett Company in 1923 across a broad hand-tool line: brad sets, center punches, clamps, countersinks, cut nippers, drill blocks, hack saws and frames, nail sets, pin vises, pliers, saws, scrapers, screw drivers, scribers, tap wrenches, vises, and wrenches. The enumerated goods are cutting, holding, and driving tools; the registration does not list rules or gauges. The mark's design codes (170701, 170708) sit in the USPTO "measuring and controlling instruments" category, so the device's imagery evokes measurement even though its registered goods do not include measuring instruments.

The Connection

Both records belong to The L.S. Starrett Company and fall within roughly half a year of each other — the steel-rule patent granted December 1922, the mark filed August 1923. The correspondence is one of shared owner and era, not a documented product link. The steel rule of US1438617A is a measuring instrument and is *not* among the hand tools the mark enumerates, so these records give no evidence that this patent was practiced under this mark. The pair's value is in showing Starrett's two parallel tracks in the early 1920s: patenting precision measuring tools while registering brand protection across its general tool catalog.

Editorial note. The free-model draft claimed the steel rule "provides the fundamental measuring component that underlies many of the listed trademarked tools, particularly those used for layout and marking such as brad sets and center punches," and it invented specific trademark classes ("Class 6" and "Class 8") not present in the record. Those statements were removed in human review: the patented rule is not among the mark's goods, and the class attributions were unsupported. The validator certifies the facts (serial, patent, dates, owner, no cross-contamination); the interpretive and class claims are not machine-checkable and were corrected by hand.

Historical Context

The L.S. Starrett Company, founded in 1880, was a leading American maker of precision measuring instruments by the interwar years. The steel-rule patent and the catalog-wide trademark both date from the early-1920s expansion of standardized machine-shop practice, when firms protected mechanical refinements by patent and registered marks to defend full product lines in a crowded tool market.

Significance

This pair, with the shortest date gap of the three Starrett pairings in this project (about 0.7 years), is a tight *temporal* correspondence between a measuring-instrument patent and a brand mark under one owner — but, read honestly, still an owner-and-era pair rather than a goods match. It demonstrates how close co-dating can be mistaken for a product-patent embodiment, and why the goods list, not the calendar, settles that question.
