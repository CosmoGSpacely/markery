# Excerpts — Tower in Babel

*Barnouw, Erik, 1966. Oxford University Press.*

## Passages

### De Forest's Audion — the triode vacuum tube

> "He added a third element or 'grid' in the vacuum tube, enormously increasing its effectiveness as detector and amplifier. The 'Audion,' as he called it, was patented in 1906." (p. 25)

Context: Documents Lee de Forest's Audion invention — the triode vacuum tube foundational to radio amplification and detection. The AUDION trademark in radio-pioneers derives from this patent. Barnouw's account is the standard scholarly reference for this invention.

### Fleming/Marconi diode patent (1904–1905)

> "In 1904 in England, Professor John Ambrose Fleming, utilizing an observation made by Edison years earlier during work on the incandescent lamp, developed a glass-bulb detector. Fleming was working for the Marconi company, which obtained the patents—in 1904 in England, in 1905 in the United States." (p. 25)

Context: Documents the Fleming valve (diode vacuum tube) and Marconi's patent control — establishing the patent thicket that preceded the RCA pool arrangement. Directly relevant to understanding why the vacuum tube patent pool was necessary.

### De Forest's broadcast vision

> "My present task (happy one) is to distribute sweet melody broadcast over the city and sea so that in time even the mariner far out across the silent waves may hear the music of his homeland . . ." (p. 25)

Context: De Forest's own articulation of the broadcast vision — relevant to the De Forest Radio Company entity and the AUDION/OSCILLION trademarks covering broadcast reception equipment.

### Crystal vs Audion — receiver technology

> "The received current was feeble but could be made audible with the help of headphones. The crystal thus became the poor man's Audion, more limited in range, erratic, but nonetheless a thing of miracles." (p. 29)

Context: Contrasts crystal and vacuum tube receivers — establishes vacuum tube superiority as the commercial driver behind RCA's branded radio set strategy and the patent pool's commercial value.

### Amateur operators as broadcasting founders

> "Many of those who started and directed radio broadcasting stations in the 1920's—and, in many cases, television stations later—were 'amateurs' in the fertile time before World War I." (p. 29)

Context: Documents the amateur-to-commercial transition that Westinghouse (KDKA) and RCA exploited — relevant to the 1920–1940 radio-pioneers time window and the emergence of commercial broadcasting.
