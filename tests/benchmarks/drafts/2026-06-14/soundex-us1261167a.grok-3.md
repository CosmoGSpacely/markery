## The Invention

US1261167A describes a phonetic indexing system that encodes surnames by consonant sound into a letter-number code, enabling retrieval of records without exact spelling. The system, later known as Soundex, converts names into a standardized code based on the first letter and subsequent consonant sounds while ignoring vowels and certain silent letters. Filed 1917-10-25 and granted 1918-04-02 to Remington Typewriter Company, the patent provided a mechanical and clerical method for improving accuracy in large card-based name indexes.

## The Mark

Trademark Serial No. 71246709 was filed 1927-03-31 by RAND KARDEX BUREAU, INC. for the mark SOUNDEX. The goods and services covered blank and partially-printed cards and forms for indexes, index guide cards, separator cards of the type used for phonetic indexes, and frames and holders for index cards. The filing positioned SOUNDEX as the commercial name for a line of phonetic index products rather than a general office supply.

## The Connection

US1261167A supplied the technical encoding method that the SOUNDEX-marked cards and forms were designed to implement. The 1918 patent's consonant-based coding procedure directly enabled the phonetic retrieval function claimed in the 1927 trademark goods description. Although nine years separate the patent grant from the trademark filing, the assignee lineage through Remington Rand and the explicit reference to phonetic indexes in the trademark record establish the correspondence between the invention and the marketed product line.

## Historical Context

In the 1910s and 1920s, American manufacturers and government agencies confronted rapidly expanding name-based record collections in insurance, employment, and census work. Card-index systems marketed by firms such as Remington Typewriter Company and its successors addressed these scale problems through standardized physical formats and retrieval codes. The SOUNDEX filing occurred during the consolidation of office-equipment producers into larger entities capable of supplying both the patented encoding method and the physical cards that embodied it.

## Significance

The pair illustrates how a single 1918 patent could anchor a distinct product identity registered nearly a decade later under a successor corporate name. It also shows the USPTO record capturing the transition from technical invention to branded index supplies within the same industrial group. The case supplies a clear instance of date-aligned, goods-claims-matched correspondence between an early mechanical patent and a later trademark for its commercial implementation.
