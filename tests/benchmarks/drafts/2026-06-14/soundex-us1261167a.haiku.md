## The Invention

US1261167A describes a phonetic indexing system that encodes surnames by consonant sound into a standardized letter-number code, enabling rapid record retrieval without dependence on exact spelling. The patent specifies an algorithmic reduction of proper names to four-character codes, allowing users to locate records by sound rather than orthography—a critical capability for large institutional and clerical filing systems where name variation and handwriting legibility created retrieval bottlenecks.

## The Mark

The SOUNDEX trademark, filed 31 March 1927 by RAND KARDEX BUREAU, INC., claimed goods including blank and partially-printed cards, index forms, guide cards, separator cards, and frames designed specifically for phonetic indexing systems. The goods description explicitly references "the type used for phonetic indexes," anchoring the mark to the technical system itself. RAND KARDEX BUREAU was the Remington Rand subsidiary handling card filing equipment and supplies—the commercial arm through which the patented system reached the market as a branded product.

## The Connection

The patent protection granted to Remington Typewriter Company in 1918 established the underlying algorithmic innovation; the SOUNDEX trademark filed nine years later by the consolidated Remington Rand organization operationalized that innovation as a complete product system. The patent describes the encoding logic; the trademark covers the physical manifestation—the specially designed cards, guides, and holders that implemented the patented method in the clerical workplace. Without the patent, competitors could have reverse-engineered the system; the trademark protected the branded product ecosystem that made the system profitable.

## Historical Context

The SOUNDEX patent emerged from the technical problems of pre-computer record management: clerical systems processing millions of names required retrieval speed and accuracy across variant spellings and transcription errors. Remington Typewriter Company, dominant in office equipment, recognized that the phonetic coding innovation extended its market beyond machines into the filing and records management sector. By 1927, when Remington Rand consolidated typewriter, card equipment, and office supplies divisions, SOUNDEX had become a flagship system for institutional clients—government agencies, insurance companies, and large corporations managing personnel and customer files. Filing the trademark in March 1927 formalized what had been a commercial practice: RAND KARDEX BUREAU was staking exclusive claim to the branded product form of the patented system.

## Significance

This pair illustrates the integration of patent-protected systems into branded product lines within the office equipment industry. The nine-year gap between patent grant (1918) and trademark filing (1927) reflects a two-stage commercialization: the patent secured legal monopoly on the technical method, but the trademark formalized the branded market identity only after the system had proven commercially viable in institutional markets. For the research project, SOUNDEX demonstrates how a single technical innovation could generate both patent and trademark protection in different legal regimes, and how trademark registration lagged commercial adoption by years—the product existed and was sold before the mark was formally registered.
