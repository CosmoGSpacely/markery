## The Invention

US Patent 1261167A describes a phonetic indexing system that encodes surnames by consonant sound into a letter-number code, enabling retrieval of records without exact spelling. The system, known as Soundex, systematizes what had been manual and error-prone name lookup by reducing surnames to a standardized formula that groups phonetically similar names under a single index entry.

## The Mark

The SOUNDEX trademark, filed by Rand Kardex Bureau, Inc. on 1927-03-31, names a line of indexing products: blank and partially-printed cards and forms, index guide cards, separator cards, and frames for holding index cards. The goods description explicitly identifies these as products "of the type used for phonetic indexes," anchoring the mark to the method rather than to generic card stock. The trademark classifies the Soundex system as a material product line rather than as an abstract method.

## The Connection

The patent creates the indexing method; the trademark embodies it in commercial form. US Patent 1261167A granted to Remington Typewriter Company in 1918 established the consonant-sound encoding algorithm. When Rand Kardex Bureau, Inc.—the merged successor entity—registered SOUNDEX as a trademark nine years later, the mark protected a product that *implements* that patented algorithm. The index cards, guide cards, and separator cards sold under the SOUNDEX mark carried printed reference tables, instructions, or structural features derived directly from the patented encoding system. The trademark signaled to office managers and records custodians that this product line embodied a branded, rationalized method for name retrieval.

## Historical Context

The registration comes at the crest of the 1920s efficiency movement and the rationalization of clerical labor. Rand Kardex Bureau emerged from the 1927 merger of the Kardex Bureau (founded 1906) with Remington Rand (itself a 1927 consolidation of Remington Typewriter and Rand Kardex). The SOUNDEX mark represents the consolidation era's effort to bundle patent-protected method with manufactured commodity. Remington Typewriter Company held the patent; Rand Kardex held the card manufacturing and distribution networks; the merger unified ownership. Filing the SOUNDEX trademark in March 1927 was a deliberate act of commercial naming following corporate reorganization.

## Significance

This pair illustrates how industrial patents became brand properties during the rationalization era. The nine-year gap between patent grant (1918) and trademark filing (1927) reflects the time required for a technical innovation to mature into a manufactured and marketed product line—and the time required for corporate restructuring to place patent and product distribution under unified ownership. SOUNDEX demonstrates that Remington Rand did not license or sell the technology; it integrated the patented method into its own product catalog. The explicit reference to "phonetic indexes" in the trademark goods description shows that brand name and patent claim remained tightly coupled in commercial communication: SOUNDEX meant both the technical system *and* the cards that implemented it.
