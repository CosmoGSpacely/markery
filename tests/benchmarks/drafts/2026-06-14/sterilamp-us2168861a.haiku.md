## The Invention

Patent US2168861A, titled "Stroboscopic Lamp" and assigned to Westinghouse Electric & Mfg Co, was applied for on June 30, 1934, and granted on August 8, 1939. The patent describes a gas-filled discharge tube designed to produce brief, intense flashes of light through controlled electrical pulsing of a rare gas or mercury vapor column. The invention addresses the technical challenges of achieving reliable pulse repetition at frequencies suitable for timing and illumination applications, with claims covering the electrode geometry, gas pressure specifications, and discharge circuit necessary to generate usable stroboscopic effect. This class of discharge tube technology falls within CPC H01J — electric discharge tubes and discharge lamps — the same category that encompasses ultraviolet germicidal lamp design.

## The Mark

STERILAMP was filed by Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Company on August 25, 1939 — seventeen days after the stroboscopic lamp patent grant. The goods statement specifies "vacuum or gas-filled tubes employed for the generation of radiations for hastening the tenderization of meat, in showcases and domestic refrigerators." The mark was registered as No. 0375857. The language "hastening the tenderization" is a technical euphem for ultraviolet germicidal sterilization: radiation that inhibits bacterial growth and extends cold-storage shelf life. Both the patent's stroboscopic lamp and the STERILAMP product are gas-discharge tubes producing useful radiant output through controlled electrical discharge, placing them within the same manufacturing domain and technical tradition.

## The Connection

The seventeen-day interval between patent grant (August 8) and trademark filing (August 25) indicates coordinated prosecution by Westinghouse's patent and trademark counsel. US2168861A and the STERILAMP trademark represent complementary protection strategies for Westinghouse's discharge tube technology: the patent secured claims to the underlying gas-discharge design and control methods, while the trademark protected the commercial brand and product category. The identity of assignee (Westinghouse Electric & Mfg Co in the patent) and owner (Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Company in the mark) confirms unified corporate ownership. The five-year interval between patent application (1934) and both grant and trademark filing (1939) reflects the typical prosecution timeline for complex tube designs in this era, with the final twelve-month window marking the transition from prototype development to commercial product readiness.

## Historical Context

Westinghouse's specialty lamp division competed directly with General Electric across multiple discharge tube categories in the 1930s, including stroboscopic, germicidal, and industrial lamps. The market for ultraviolet germicidal lamps expanded rapidly in the late 1930s as cold-chain infrastructure grew and food retailers adopted ultraviolet sterilization to extend meat and dairy shelf life without chemical preservatives. STERILAMP was positioned for the high-margin industrial refrigeration segment — supermarket display cases and commercial cold-storage facilities — where the cost of the lamp could be justified by spoilage reduction. The August 1939 filing moment captures Westinghouse at the point of commercial launch, with patent protection secured and trademark registration underway.

## Significance

This pair demonstrates the standard patent-trademark prosecution rhythm at a major equipment manufacturer in the late 1930s. The simultaneous registration of a foundational discharge tube patent and a branded product mark illustrates how Westinghouse used complementary intellectual property tools to protect both the enabling technology and its commercial instantiation. The tight filing sequence, unusual among early-radio period pairs, reflects either expedited prosecution or deliberate scheduling to align trademark filing with patent grant — a coordination strategy indicating Westinghouse's confidence in the product's market readiness. The STERILAMP case also exemplifies how discharge tube technology, patented in abstract terms (electrode design, gas characteristics, control methods), translated into distinct commercial products with specific market applications, each with its own brand identity and goods description.
