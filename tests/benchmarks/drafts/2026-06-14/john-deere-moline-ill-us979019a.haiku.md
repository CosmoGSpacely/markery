## The Invention

US Patent 979019A describes a reversible-disk plow, a mechanical design permitting the plow to function effectively when drawn across a field in either direction without manual repositioning. Granted to Deere & Co in December 1910, the patent addressed a practical constraint in moldboard plow operation: traditional single-direction plows required either the farmer to guide the implement along the field's edge or accept inefficient return passes. The reversible disk mechanism reduced labor demand and accelerated field preparation—a material gain for large-scale grain and hay operations across the American Midwest.

## The Mark

Deere & Company filed the mark "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." in April 1911, four months following the patent grant. The mark invoked the company's manufacturing headquarters and the founder's surname, a dual-register strategy that anchored brand identity to both place and person. The goods description—plows, cultivators, harrows, mowers, reapers, grain harvesters, hay-rakes, hay-presses, hay-tedders, hay-loaders, and hay-stackers—indicates the mark functioned as a house brand spanning Deere's entire implement catalog, not as a mark for a single product line.

## The Connection

The reversible-disk plow patent and the trademark goods description correspond directly: plows appear as the lead item in the goods specification, and the patent's core innovation—the reversible mechanism—would have been integrated into the plow implements marketed under the "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." brand. The filing date proximity (patent December 1910, trademark April 1911) reflects administrative practice at Deere: trademark registration proceeded swiftly after major patent issuance, securing the commercial identity of the branded line once the technical claim had been validated through patent grant.

## Historical Context

The 1910–1911 period marked intense consolidation in American agricultural implement manufacture. Deere & Company, headquartered in Moline since the 1870s, had established the city as the primary center of plow innovation and large-scale production. Patent activity during this interval reflected Deere's strategy of iterative mechanical refinement—dozens of design modifications to disk, moldboard, and reversing mechanisms filed across the decade—coupled with protective trademark registration for the branded implements themselves. This pair exemplifies how manufacturing firms treated patent and trademark registration as complementary: patents protected the technical novelty; trademarks protected the brand association with that novelty.

## Significance

This pair confirms the correspondence between a specific patented agricultural mechanism and the trademark house brand under which implements embodying that mechanism were sold to farmers. The evidence supports classification of the reversible-disk plow as a Deere innovation actively marketed under Deere brand identity in the 1911–1920 period. For the research project, the pair demonstrates how American capital-goods manufacturers used patent-plus-trademark strategy to consolidate both technical and commercial position in regional implement markets.
