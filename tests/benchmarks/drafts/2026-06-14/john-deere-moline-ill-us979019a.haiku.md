## The Invention

US Patent 979019A describes a reversible-disk plow—a mechanical innovation enabling farmers to turn soil in either direction without repositioning the implement. Assigned to Deere & Co and granted on 1910-12-20, the patent solved a persistent inefficiency in moldboard plow operation: conventional designs worked productively in only one direction across a field. The reversible mechanism reduced repositioning labor and accelerated field preparation, a material advantage for large-scale agricultural producers where time-to-harvest directly affected profitability.

## The Mark

The trademark "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." was filed by Deere & Company in April 1911, approximately four months after the patent issued. The mark combined the founder's surname with the company's manufacturing location—Moline, Illinois, then the dominant center of American plow production—functioning as both geographic identifier and corporate brand. The goods specification lists a comprehensive cross-section of Deere's implement catalog: plows, cultivators, harrows, mowers, reapers, grain harvesters, hay-rakes, hay-presses, and stacking equipment. This breadth indicates the mark functioned as a house brand encompassing the full agricultural implement line rather than a single product designation.

## The Connection

The reversible-disk plow patent directly corresponds to the plow goods enumerated in the trademark registration. The patent describes a technical feature—the reversible disk mechanism—that would have been incorporated into plows manufactured and sold under the "JOHN DEERE" brand identity from Moline. The temporal proximity of the patent grant (December 1910) and trademark filing (April 1911) suggests deliberate coordination: Deere filed the mark to secure commercial identity protection once the patent issued, consolidating both technical claim and brand registration in the same goods category.

## Historical Context

This pair reflects Deere's systematic approach to plow innovation during the 1910s: continuous technical refinement coupled with rapid trademark registration to protect brand position. Moline had become the primary center of American plow design and manufacture by 1910, with Deere leading a concentration of implement makers in the region. The reversible-disk plow was one of numerous incremental innovations—many patented, many registered—through which Deere maintained competitive differentiation in a market where dozens of regional manufacturers competed on both price and feature sets.

## Significance

This pairing demonstrates the coordination of patent and trademark strategy in agricultural implement manufacture. The close dating confirms that major patent issuances functioned as triggers for trademark action—brand registration timed to capitalize on technical validation and protect the commercial identity of product lines incorporating the patented feature. For the research project, the pair illuminates how capital-goods manufacturers with strong regional identity used patent protection for technical features and trademark registration for brand consolidation in a competitive market dominated by a small number of large producers.
