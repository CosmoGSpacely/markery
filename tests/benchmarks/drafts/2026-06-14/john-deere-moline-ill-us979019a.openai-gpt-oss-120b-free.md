## The Invention

US Patent 979019A describes a reversible-disk plow—a mechanical innovation that allowed farmers to turn the soil in either direction without repositioning the implement. The patent, assigned to Deere & Co and granted in December 1910, addressed a practical problem in moldboard plow design: the inefficiency of plows that worked in only one direction across a field. This advancement reduced labor and improved the speed of field preparation, a significant consideration for large-scale agricultural operations.

## The Mark

The trademark "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." was filed by Deere & Company in April 1911, less than four months after the plow patent issued. The mark named the company's location—Moline, Illinois, the center of American plow manufacturing—and carried the founder's name as brand identifier. The goods description spans the full breadth of Deere's agricultural implement line: plows, cultivators, harrows, mowers, reapers, grain harvesters, and an extensive roster of haying equipment, indicating that this mark served as a comprehensive house brand for the company's entire machinery division.

## The Connection

The reversible-disk plow patent directly underlies the plow goods named in the trademark registration. The patent's technical innovation—the reversible mechanism—was incorporated into implements marketed under the "JOHN DEERE" brand from Moline. The proximity of the filing dates (patent granted December 1910; trademark filed April 1911) suggests Deere moved swiftly to register the mark as protection for its line once the patent issued, securing both technical claim and commercial identity in the same trademark class.

## Historical Context

This pair exemplifies Deere's strategy during the 1910s: rapid iteration in plow design, coupled with aggressive trademark registration to protect the brand identity. Moline, Illinois had become the epicenter of plow innovation and manufacture in the American Midwest by 1910. The reversible-disk plow was one of dozens of incremental innovations Deere pursued to maintain competitive advantage in a crowded market where dozens of regional manufacturers competed on both price and technical feature.

## Significance

This pair demonstrates how agricultural implement manufacturers used patent protection for technical features and trademark registration for brand consolidation. The close dating confirms that Deere treated major patent issuances as triggers for trademark action—protecting the commercial identity of the product line at the moment of technical validation. For the research project, this pair illustrates the relationship between innovation cadence and brand registration in a capital‑goods industry dominated by a small number of large manufacturers with strong regional identity.
