## The Invention

US Patent 979019A describes a reversible‑disk plow—a mechanical innovation that allowed farmers to turn the soil in either direction without repositioning the implement. The patent, assigned to Deere & Co and granted on 1910-12-20, addressed a practical problem in moldboard plow design: the inefficiency of plows that worked in only one direction across a field. This advancement reduced labor and improved the speed of field preparation, a significant consideration for large‑scale agricultural operations.

## The Mark

The trademark “JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL.” was filed by Deere & Company on 1911-04-08, less than four months after the plow patent issued. The mark names the company’s manufacturing base in Moline, Illinois, and incorporates the founder’s surname as a brand identifier. The goods description enumerates the full breadth of Deere’s agricultural implement line—plows, cultivators, harrows, mowers, reapers, grain harvesters, and an extensive roster of hay‑handling equipment—indicating that the registration functioned as a house brand for the entire machinery division.

## The Connection

The reversible‑disk plow claimed in Patent 979019A directly underlies the plow category listed in the trademark registration. The patent’s reversible mechanism was incorporated into implements marketed under the “JOHN DEERE” name from Moline, and the close temporal proximity of the grant (1910‑12‑20) and the trademark filing (1911‑04‑08) suggests that Deere moved swiftly to secure brand protection concurrent with the technical disclosure. This alignment of dates supports the inference that the trademark was intended to protect the commercial identity of the newly patented plow design.

## Historical Context

During the 1910s Deere pursued a strategy of rapid, incremental innovation in plow design while simultaneously expanding its trademark portfolio to safeguard brand equity. By 1910 Moline, Illinois had become the epicenter of American plow manufacturing, and Deere’s reversible‑disk plow was one of dozens of technical refinements introduced to maintain a competitive edge in a market populated by numerous regional manufacturers. The comprehensive goods description in the 1911 filing reflects Deere’s intent to cover its entire line of implements under a single, location‑based house mark.

## Significance

This pair illustrates how major agricultural implement firms combined patent protection for specific technical features with trademark registration for broader brand consolidation. The narrow 0.3‑year gap between the patent grant and the trademark filing demonstrates that Deere treated the issuance of a substantive patent as a trigger for immediate brand registration, thereby protecting both the invention and its commercial identity in the same trademark class. For the research project, the case exemplifies the cadence of innovation and brand protection characteristic of capital‑goods manufacturers in the early twentieth‑century United States.
