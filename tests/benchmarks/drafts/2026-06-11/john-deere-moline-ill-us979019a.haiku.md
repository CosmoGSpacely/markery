## The Invention

US Patent 979019A claims a reversible-disk plow mechanism permitting operation in either direction without repositioning the implement in the field. The patent, assigned to Deere & Co and granted December 20, 1910, targeted a recognized inefficiency in moldboard plow operation: directional dependency that forced repositioning or compromise in field coverage. The reversible design reduced setup time and labor per acre, a material advantage in mechanized agriculture where implement efficiency directly affected farm profitability and competitive viability.

## The Mark

The trademark "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." was filed April 8, 1911 by Deere & Company—approximately four months after patent issuance. The mark explicitly claims the company's manufacturing location (Moline, Illinois) alongside the founder's name as the commercial identity, a strategy that tied brand reputation directly to place of origin. The goods specification itemizes the complete product category: plows, cultivators, harrows, mowers, reapers, grain harvesters, binders, hay-rakes, hay-presses, hay-tedders, hay-loaders, and hay-stackers, demonstrating that "JOHN DEERE" functioned as a comprehensive house mark across Deere's entire agricultural machinery portfolio.

## The Connection

The reversible-disk plow patent describes a specific technical feature incorporated into the plow implements explicitly named in the trademark goods description. Patent US979019A's claims address the operational mechanism; the trademark registration secures the commercial identity under which that mechanism would be marketed. The four-month interval between patent grant and trademark filing suggests deliberate sequencing: Deere waited for patent issuance to proceed with registration, establishing a unified legal position covering both the innovation (patent) and its commercial embodiment (trademark).

## Historical Context

By 1911, Moline, Illinois had consolidated its position as the dominant American plow manufacturing center, competing fiercely against John Case (Racine), Massey-Harris (Ontario), and numerous smaller regional makers. The reversible-disk plow was one element of Deere's ongoing technical optimization strategy—incremental but materially significant design improvements that differentiated product lines in a market where farmers compared implements on feature specificity as well as price. Patent applications by Deere during this period cluster in plow mechanics, harrow design, and haying equipment, indicating systematic innovation investment across the full implement line.

## Significance

This pair documents how agricultural equipment manufacturers deployed patent and trademark protection as complementary commercial instruments during the mechanization era. The tight dating and complete entity continuity (same assignee/owner, identical goods categories) confirm direct instrumental correspondence: the patent describes technical innovation; the trademark registers the commercial identity under which that innovation was sold. For agricultural implement history, this pair exemplifies how market leaders maintained competitive position through continuous design improvement coupled with aggressive brand protection at the moment of technical validation.
