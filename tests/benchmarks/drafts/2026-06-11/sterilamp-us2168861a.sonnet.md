## The Invention

Patent US2168861A, titled "Stroboscopic Lamp" and assigned to Westinghouse Electric & Mfg Co, was applied for on June 30, 1934, and granted on August 8, 1939 — a prosecution period of over five years, consistent with the technical complexity and competitive landscape of discharge lamp development in that decade. The patent falls within CPC class H01J, which governs electric discharge tubes and discharge lamps, and addresses the design of gas-filled tubes capable of producing brief, intense pulses of radiant energy through controlled electrical discharge. Achieving reliable stroboscopic performance required precise specification of gas composition and pressure, electrode geometry, and discharge circuit parameters to govern pulse duration and spectral characteristics. That engineering specificity — the controlled manipulation of discharge conditions to produce a defined radiant output — is the technical core that connects this patent to the STERILAMP product.

## The Mark

STERILAMP (Trademark Serial No. 71423019) was filed by Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Company on August 25, 1939, seventeen days after the grant of US2168861A. The goods description identifies the covered articles as "vacuum or gas-filled tubes employed for the generation of radiations for hastening the tenderization of meat, in showcases and domestic refrigerators" — a formulation that locates the product precisely within the food-retail and cold-storage trades rather than the broader industrial or laboratory lamp market. The mark was subsequently registered as No. 0375857. The STERILAMP name itself performs commercial work: it condenses the product's germicidal function into a single coinage that would be legible to a meat retailer or cold-storage operator without requiring technical explanation of ultraviolet radiation or discharge tube physics.

## The Connection

US2168861A and the STERILAMP trademark are connected through a shared underlying technology class — H01J discharge tubes — and through the unified Westinghouse engineering and manufacturing organization that produced both. Stroboscopic lamps and ultraviolet germicidal lamps are distinct commercial products with distinct end uses, but they are variants on the same electrophysical principle: a sealed envelope containing gas or vapor, through which electrical discharge is induced to generate radiant energy of a controlled spectral character. The stroboscopic patent, applied for in 1934, documents Westinghouse's active development of discharge tube design across the mid-1930s; the STERILAMP trademark filing of August 25, 1939, captures the moment a parallel product from that same research and manufacturing base entered branded commerce. The seventeen-day interval between patent grant and trademark filing is more consistent with coordinated prosecution strategy than with coincidence, and suggests that Westinghouse's patent and trademark counsel were working in close communication as this product line reached market readiness.

## Historical Context

By 1939, Westinghouse occupied a leading position in specialty lamp technology, competing directly with General Electric across incandescent, fluorescent, and discharge lamp categories. Public health interest in food safety, amplified by the growth of the supermarket trade and commercial cold-storage infrastructure in the 1930s, created a receptive market for germicidal lamp products positioned as hygienic solutions for meat display and refrigerated storage. Westinghouse's decision to register STERILAMP for showcase and refrigerator applications — rather than for the broader bacteriological or medical markets where UV lamps were also in use — reflects a deliberate targeting of the food-retail trade, where purchasing decisions were made by store operators and cold-storage contractors rather than by laboratories or hospitals. The August 1939 filing date places the commercial launch of the STERILAMP line at the close of the decade that had established ultraviolet germicidal technology as a viable industrial product category.

## Significance

This pair illustrates how a large, vertically integrated manufacturer like Westinghouse used coordinated patent and trademark prosecution to secure simultaneous protection over the technical invention and the commercial identity of a product at the moment of market entry. The 17-day gap between the grant of US2168861A on August 8, 1939, and the trademark filing of August 25, 1939, is among the tightest date alignments in the candidate record, and it is difficult to read as anything other than deliberate scheduling. The pairing also demonstrates the range of commercial applications Westinghouse was extracting from its discharge tube technology base in this period: the same H01J engineering platform that underpinned stroboscopic timing instruments, radio transmitter tubes, and fluorescent lighting was simultaneously being commercialized in the form of a branded germicidal lamp sold to butchers and cold-storage operators — a reminder that the boundaries between industrial, consumer, and scientific markets were considerably more porous in Westinghouse's product strategy than a category-by-category reading of the filing record might suggest.
