## The Invention

US1261167A, applied 1917-10-25 and granted 1918-04-02, describes a phonetic indexing system that encodes surnames by their consonant-sound structure into a compact letter-number code, allowing records to be located without knowledge of exact spelling. The system groups phonetically similar consonants under shared numerical values, so that variant spellings of the same surname — whether the variation arises from transcription error, immigration transliteration, or dialect — collapse to a single retrievable code. The assignee of record at grant was Remington Typewriter Company.

## The Mark

Serial No. 71246709, filed 1927-03-31 by Rand Kardex Bureau, Inc., registered the word mark SOUNDEX against a goods description that names the physical apparatus of a phonetic index: blank and partially-printed cards and forms, index guide cards, separator cards specifically described as being of the type used for phonetic indexes, and the frames and holders designed to contain them. The goods description makes explicit that SOUNDEX was not a generic office-supply line but a named system — the separator cards and guide cards are identified by their phonetic-index function, not merely by their material form. The mark thus named a complete commercial product: the pre-printed card stock, the structural guides, and the hardware that together instantiated the patented encoding method as a purchasable indexing system.

## The Connection

The goods description in Serial No. 71246709 tracks directly onto the operative requirements of US1261167A. The patent's phonetic encoding system only functions as a retrieval tool when records are organized according to its code structure, which in turn requires index cards printed with the appropriate alphabetic and numeric fields, separator cards keyed to code values, and holders that maintain the coded sequence. The trademarked product line supplied precisely those physical components: the "INDEX GUIDE CARDS AND SEPARATOR CARDS OF THE TYPE USED FOR PHONETIC INDEXES" named in the goods description are the material embodiment of the code structure the patent defines. Without pre-formatted card stock aligned to the Soundex encoding scheme, the patented method would remain an abstraction; the trademarked goods converted it into a deployable office system.

## Historical Context

The nine-year interval between the patent grant (1918-04-02) and the trademark filing (1927-03-31) corresponds to a period of significant corporate reorganization in the American office-equipment industry. Remington Typewriter Company, the patent assignee at grant, was absorbed into the consolidation that produced Remington Rand, Inc. in 1927 — the same year the SOUNDEX mark was filed — with Rand Kardex Bureau, Inc. representing one of the constituent entities of that merger. The filing of the mark in 1927-03-31 therefore appears to coincide with, or immediately anticipate, the formal consolidation: the act of trademarking SOUNDEX under the Rand Kardex name can be read as an assertion of commercial ownership over the phonetic-index product line at the moment the corporate structure was being rationalized. The timing suggests that the trademark filing was as much a housekeeping instrument in a merger as it was a new market entry.

## Significance

This pair offers a relatively rare case in which the goods description of a trademark filing names the patented method it commercializes with sufficient specificity to establish the correspondence on documentary grounds alone — the phrase "of the type used for phonetic indexes" in the goods description is not generic, and it maps directly onto the subject matter of US1261167A. The pair also illustrates a structural feature of the early office-equipment industry: the gap between a patent's technical disclosure and the moment of formal brand registration frequently reflects corporate transaction rather than product latency, with the trademark arriving not when the product was first sold but when ownership needed to be consolidated and publicly asserted. For the research project, SOUNDEX — US1261167A thus serves as a model case for reading date gaps as corporate-history evidence rather than as evidence of delayed commercialization.
