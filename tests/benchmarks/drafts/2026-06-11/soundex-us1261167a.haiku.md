## The Invention

US Patent 1261167A, granted to Remington Typewriter Company on April 2, 1918, describes a phonetic indexing system that encodes surnames by consonant sound into a letter-number code, enabling rapid retrieval of records without exact spelling knowledge. The system translates surnames into a standardized four-character code (one letter followed by three digits) based on phonetic similarity, solving a critical problem in manual record management: the inability to locate names when spelling was uncertain or when similar-sounding variants existed in the same file.

## The Mark

RAND KARDEX BUREAU, INC. registered the mark SOUNDEX on March 31, 1927 (Registration No. 230958) for a complete line of physical indexing products: blank and partially-printed cards and forms, pre-constructed indexes on cards, index guide cards, separator cards, and frames and holders for card storage. The goods description explicitly identifies these as products "of the type used for phonetic indexes," making the connection to the patent's invention explicit in the filing itself. The mark named not a single product but an entire system — the tangible, manufactured instantiation of the phonetic coding method.

## The Connection

The patent's phonetic encoding algorithm became the organizing principle for Rand Kardex's index card system and supporting materials. By 1927, when the trademark was filed, Rand Kardex had commercialized the patent invention into a branded product line: pre-printed cards with the Soundex code structure already built in, guide cards organized by phonetic code, and storage frames designed to accommodate the system. The mark SOUNDEX transformed a patented method into a product category. Customers purchasing Soundex cards and frames were purchasing pre-fabricated instantiations of the 1918 patent — they could organize records phonetically without needing to understand or independently apply the coding algorithm.

## Historical Context

The filing gap of nine years between the 1918 patent grant and the March 1927 trademark filing reflects the delay inherent in commercializing an indexing innovation during the postwar period and through the consolidation of office equipment manufacturers. Remington Typewriter Company held the patent; by 1927, Rand Kardex Bureau, Inc. — likely a subsidiary or affiliated division within the emerging Remington Rand conglomerate — held the trademark and manufactured the product. The mid-1920s saw intensifying competition in office mechanization: filing systems, card indexes, and information retrieval were central to the rationalization of clerical labor. Trademarking SOUNDEX in 1927 staked a proprietary claim to a specific phonetic filing method precisely as the office products market was consolidating.

## Significance

This pair illustrates the translation of patent-protected method into branded product category. SOUNDEX is not a patent number or an inventor's name; it is a mark that enabled consumers to purchase a commercialized system without understanding or licensing the underlying patented method. The explicit reference in the goods description to "phonetic indexes" confirms that the trademark was filed to protect not merely the word but the association between the mark and a specific, patented technological approach to record retrieval. For the research project, this pair demonstrates how industrial firms leveraged both patent protection and trademark registration to build durable market advantages: the patent secured exclusivity in the method; the mark secured exclusivity in the name and the manufactured products that embodied the method.
