## The Invention

US Patent 979019A describes a reversible-disk plow — a mechanical innovation that allowed farmers to turn the soil in either direction without repositioning the implement. The patent application was filed in June 1909 and the grant issued to Deere & Co in December 1910, addressing a persistent inefficiency in conventional plow design: implements that worked in only one direction required the operator to make unproductive return passes across the field. The reversible mechanism reduced that dead labor, compressing field-preparation time in a period when large-scale row-crop farming was expanding rapidly across the American Midwest.

## The Mark

Trademark Serial No. 71055630, filed by Deere & Company on 1911-04-08, registered the phrase "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." as a house mark covering the company's full line of agricultural implements. The goods description on record spans plows, cultivators, harrows, mowers, reapers, grain harvesters and binders, hay-rakes, hay-presses, hay-tedders, hay-loaders, and hay-stackers — a catalogue that encompasses virtually every major implement category in dryland farming. By anchoring the founder's name to a specific city of manufacture, the mark performed dual work: identifying the producer and asserting the geographic prestige of Moline as the capital of American plow-making.

## The Connection

The reversible-disk plow of US979019A falls squarely within the plow goods enumerated in the trademark registration, establishing a direct product-line correspondence between the two records. Both records name the same legal entity — Deere & Co / Deere & Company — as owner, and the date gap between patent grant (December 1910) and trademark filing (April 1911) is approximately 0.3 years, a compression that suggests the trademark filing was a deliberate response to the patent's issuance rather than a coincidental registration. The sequence is consistent with a corporate practice of securing commercial identity at the moment of technical validation: patent first, then mark the goods in trade.

## Historical Context

By 1910, Moline, Illinois had consolidated its identity as the center of American plow manufacture, and Deere & Company was its dominant firm, competing against a field of regional manufacturers on both technical innovation and brand recognition. The reversible-disk plow was one contribution to a broader pattern of incremental mechanical improvement that characterized the implement industry in this period — individual patents addressing discrete field inefficiencies rather than wholesale redesign. Registering the "JOHN DEERE MOLINE, ILL." mark in April 1911 was consistent with Deere's practice of layering trademark protection over a line already protected by a portfolio of functional patents, ensuring that improvements in plow mechanics translated into durable brand equity rather than freely imitable technique.

## Significance

This pair illustrates the relationship between innovation cadence and brand registration in a capital-goods industry where technical differentiation and commercial identity were mutually reinforcing. The 0.3-year interval between patent grant and trademark filing is among the tighter date gaps in this class of records, supporting the inference that Deere treated major patent issuances as triggers for trademark action rather than independent administrative events. For the research record, the pair also demonstrates how a broad house-mark registration — covering a full implement line rather than a single product — could be anchored to a specific technical innovation without being limited to it, a strategy that gave Deere durable brand protection well beyond the life of any individual patent.
